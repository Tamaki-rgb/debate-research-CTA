Does Debate Training Develop Critical Thinking?

A data-driven study examining the relationship between debate participation and critical thinking skills among Kazakhstani secondary school students.

01 — About

The Research Question

Why This Matters

Debate is widely used as an educational tool, yet few data-driven studies examine its actual impact on students' reasoning abilities — especially in Central Asian educational contexts.

What We Investigated

We surveyed students with and without debate experience, measuring their critical thinking across four distinct cognitive dimensions using a structured, scenario-based questionnaire.

02 — Methodology

How the Study Was Conducted

Data was collected through an anonymous online survey distributed across secondary schools in Shymkent, Kazakhstan. Respondents were divided into two groups based on their debate participation history.

01

Survey Design

16 questions across three blocks: background, scenario-based tasks, and self-assessment. Criteria based on the Watson-Glaser Critical Thinking model.

02

Data Collection

100 students surveyed anonymously via Google Forms. Respondents split into two groups: debate participants and non-participants.

03

Analysis

Responses were analyzed using Python (pandas, matplotlib). Groups were compared across all four critical thinking criteria with percentage scoring.

03 — Criteria

What We Measured

Critical thinking was broken down into four measurable dimensions, each assessed through real-world scenario questions.

🎯

Argumentation

Ability to support a position with concrete, evidence-based reasoning rather than opinion or anecdote.

🔍

Source Evaluation

Capacity to distinguish reliable sources from unreliable ones and identify weak logical foundations.

🔄

Openness to Change

Willingness to revise one's position when presented with new, credible evidence or strong counter-arguments.

🧩

Structured Thinking

Ability to approach problems systematically, identify root causes, and avoid logical fallacies.

05 — Conclusions

Key Findings

The data reveals clear and consistent differences between students with and without debate experience across all four critical thinking criteria.

Debate participants outperformed non-participants in Source Evaluation (65.8% vs 33.1%) and Openness to Change (63.2% vs 33.9%), suggesting debate training builds analytical habits.

Structured Thinking showed the largest advantage for debate participants (72.8% vs 43.0%), indicating that structured argumentation practice improves logical problem-solving.

Non-participants scored unexpectedly higher on Argumentation (18.5% vs 7.9%) — a counterintuitive result that warrants further investigation and may reflect question framing effects.

Self-assessed scores diverged significantly from actual performance, revealing a consistent overconfidence gap among non-participants.

These findings support the case for broader integration of debate programs into Kazakhstani secondary school curricula as a structured critical thinking intervention.
